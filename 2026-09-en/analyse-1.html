Rates are set to stay higher for longer

The Fed, the ECB and the Bank of Japan raised rates in September. Bond markets followed, while equities remained calm.

A year ago, the Fed was cutting rates. In September it raised them for the first time since 2023, unanimously, to 4.00%, and its members no longer project any cut before the end of 2027. The ECB had led the way on the 10th, lifting its deposit rate to 2.50%, also unanimously, and the Bank of Japan followed on the 18th, moving to 1.25% by seven votes to two. Only the Bank of England, which held at 3.75% despite three votes for a hike, and the SNB, which kept its rate at 0%, stood pat. In our view these hikes are open to question: higher rates do nothing to increase oil supply and mainly risk slowing growth. Central bankers counter that they must prevent inflation from spreading to services, where prices are already accelerating, and they were no doubt also keen to reassure bond investors of their willingness to act.

Bond markets took note. The US 10-year yield rose from 4.75% to 5.29%, a level last seen in 2007, on the eve of the Global Financial Crisis, and the , which tracks central bank expectations closely, climbed by the same amount: monetary policy had taken over from the fiscal concerns that drove August. Equities, by contrast, stayed calm, with the , the gauge of expected volatility on Wall Street, ending the month around 17, below its long-term average. Equity markets can absorb this rise in rates for now, but would struggle to withstand a sustained move to higher levels.

For investors

In practical terms, every new bond now offers a higher yield, and we are capturing it through short- to intermediate-dated maturities, which higher rates penalise only modestly.

Looking ahead

The Fed and ECB meetings on 28 and 29 October will show whether this tightening cycle continues. A central bank policy error is one of the three risks we are monitoring most closely, alongside the price of refined energy products and equity volatility that could eventually catch up with the stress in bond markets.

More technical background, to open as you wish.

Why the two-year yield tracks central banksWhat short and long maturities measure

The yield on a two-year bond largely reflects the policy rates investors expect over the next two years: when a central bank raises its rate, or signals that it will, this yield rises almost immediately.

The 10-year yield also responds to policy rates, but it incorporates other factors, such as expected inflation over a long period and the premium investors demand for lending to a heavily indebted government for many years. In August, the rise came mainly from long maturities, against a backdrop of fiscal concerns. In September, two- and 10-year yields rose by the same amount, a sign that monetary policy was driving the move.

Why short-dated bonds suffer little from rising ratesDuration in practice

When rates rise, the price of existing bonds falls, since new issues offer a higher return. The size of that fall depends on the remaining maturity: as a rule of thumb, a one-point rise in rates costs a two-year bond around 2% and a 10-year bond 8% to 9%.

A short-dated bond also matures quickly, and the proceeds can be reinvested at the new, higher rate. That is why we are concentrating our bond holdings on short- to intermediate-dated maturities in the current environment.
