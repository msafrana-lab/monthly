1Midterm elections

The Democrats regain Congress after a favourable year for equities

19 out of 19Midterm elections since 1950 followed by a rise in US equities over the next twelve months.

On 3 November, the Democrats regained the House of Representatives and, against most expectations, almost certainly the Senate. Donald Trump will therefore have to work with an opposition Congress, which makes further tax cuts or spending increases unlikely. Since 1950, US equities have often fallen before these elections, as investors could not tell which policies would apply next, then risen in the twelve months after each of the nineteen votes. In 2026, that decline never came. At its worst, early in the war in Iran, the S&P 500 lost only 9%.

Looking aheadThe usual rebound therefore has less room. The president may rely more on tools that do not require Congress, such as tariffs, but what follows will depend mainly on corporate earnings and energy prices.

2Oil

Oil prices reflect fears of a renewed conflict with Iran

USD 114Brent per barrel on 23 October, up to a five-month high.

In early October, the US press reported that the White House had asked the military for strike options against Iran. President Donald Trump then gave assurances that no attack would take place before the vote. Brent rose to 114 dollars a barrel on 23 October, even though Gulf exports had almost returned to pre-war levels. In our view, supply and demand justify 90 to 95 dollars; the remaining 20 dollars or so compensate for the risk of a new conflict.

Looking aheadNow that the vote is over, this premium could disappear quickly if tensions ease. Investors are sceptical. The price of oil for delivery at the end of 2027 rose sharply in October. We are therefore keeping assets able to cushion an energy shock.

3Artificial intelligence

Technology giants must show what their AI spending will earn

USD 790 bnPlanned investment in 2026 by the five largest AI players.

From 6 October, investors sold companies linked to artificial intelligence (AI), starting with those that rent out computing power on borrowed money. On 29 October, Alphabet, Google's parent company, dropped around 12% intraday despite revenue growth of 25%, because its spending exceeded the cash generated by its operations. The sector's five largest players plan to invest close to USD 790 billion this year, more than their operations bring in, and investors now want to know when that spending will start to pay off.

Looking aheadCapital rotated towards more , and the SMI gained more than 7% on the strength of Roche and Nestlé. In portfolios, diversification across sectors and regions thus cushioned the technology sell-off.

Concept of the month
Risk premium

The extra price or return investors demand for a risk that has not yet materialised.

In October, Brent rose above 110 dollars a barrel; in our view, supply and demand justified 90 to 95. The gap is the risk premium attached to a new conflict with Iran. It can shrink quickly if that risk recedes.
