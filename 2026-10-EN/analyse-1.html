Analysis 1 of 3 · Midterm elections

The Democrats regain Congress after a favourable year for equities

The Democrats regained the House of Representatives and, more unexpectedly, the Senate. Since 1950, US equities have often fallen before midterm elections and recovered once the vote was over. In 2026, the decline never came.

On Tuesday 3 November, Americans elected the entire House of Representatives and a third of the Senate. These lines are written the following day, while votes are still being counted, but the outcome is no longer really in doubt: the Democrats have regained the House, as expected, and almost certainly the Senate, which few observers had considered likely. Before assessing the consequences, the stock market's history around deserves a closer look. Since 1950, the S&P 500 has been through nineteen of them. On average, it fell 16% between the start of the election year and polling day, then rose almost 19% on average over the following twelve months. Notably, the index ended higher after every one of these elections, with gains ranging from 4% in 1986 to 39% in 1954.

This pattern stems from the uncertainty that precedes the vote: as long as the outcome remains open, investors cannot know which tax and spending policies will apply the following year. Once the polls have closed, the president's party usually loses seats and has to deal with a less compliant Congress, so major reforms become unlikely, a gridlock that markets tend to accommodate to quite comfortably. Against this backdrop, the six months following midterm elections have historically returned an average of 16%, against 5% in other years.

The political uncertainty that precedes midterm elections weighs on the stock market, while the months following the vote have historically been more rewarding.

The year 2026, however, has departed from this pattern. Like Godot in Samuel Beckett's play, the decline typical of an election year never came: the steepest drop, in the first quarter when war broke out in Iran, was limited to 9%, and the index has set some thirty records since. A post-election rebound of historical magnitude therefore looks unlikely, even though third-quarter corporate results remain encouraging.

The vote count has also defied expectations: on the eve of the election, gave the Democrats only a two-in-three chance of winning the Senate. An opposition Congress makes any further fiscal stimulus unlikely and tightens parliamentary scrutiny of spending, which is broadly supportive of US Treasuries, although it does nothing to reduce the existing deficit. Deprived of legislative levers, Donald Trump may instead make greater use of tools that do not require Congress, such as tariffs, sanctions and foreign policy. Since 1950, a Republican president facing a Democratic Congress has produced the weakest average market return of any combination, around 7.5% a year, a figure that nonetheless remains comfortably positive. The first sessions after the vote were relatively calm, with Asian and European markets moving higher.

Looking ahead

What follows will in all likelihood depend more on corporate earnings and energy prices than on the make-up of Congress.
